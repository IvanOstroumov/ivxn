DDR5's spec-sheet bandwidth numbers look like a clear win over DDR4. Real-world impact is more nuanced than that headline number suggests.

## What actually changed between DDR4 and DDR5

<Callout>
DDR5 offers higher theoretical bandwidth than DDR4, but early DDR5 kits also had higher latency, which partially works against the bandwidth gain for latency-sensitive tasks. This gap has narrowed as DDR5 has matured, but it means the spec-sheet bandwidth number alone doesn't tell the whole real-world story.
</Callout>

## Where the difference actually shows up

- **Memory-bandwidth-sensitive workloads** — some content creation, scientific/data-heavy computing, and specific memory-intensive applications benefit more directly from DDR5's higher bandwidth.
- **Gaming** — for most titles, the CPU and GPU remain bigger bottlenecks than memory bandwidth, so the DDR4-to-DDR5 jump is often a smaller real-world gain than the spec numbers imply, though this varies by specific game and system balance.
- **General use** — negligible difference for browsing, office work, and most everyday tasks.

## The platform question matters as much as the RAM itself

Newer CPU platforms increasingly support DDR5 only, or DDR5 exclusively at higher price tiers, which means the actual decision is often less "DDR4 or DDR5 on the same platform" and more "which platform/CPU generation am I buying," with the RAM type following from that choice rather than being independently decided.

## A practical framing

| Situation | Reasonable approach |
|---|---|
| Building on a platform that only supports DDR5 | Just buy appropriate DDR5, the choice is made for you |
| Building on a platform supporting both | Check current price gap and your actual workload's memory sensitivity |
| Primarily gaming | Don't over-prioritize RAM speed over CPU/GPU budget |
| Memory-bandwidth-sensitive work (rendering, some scientific computing) | DDR5's bandwidth advantage is more likely to matter |

## The honest takeaway

DDR5 is the forward-looking choice and the one new platforms increasingly assume, but the performance gap for a large share of common use cases (especially gaming) is smaller in practice than the marketing bandwidth numbers suggest. Don't let a RAM-type decision eat into budget that would improve your CPU or GPU more for your actual workload.
