TDP shows up on every CPU spec sheet and gets read as "how much power this chip uses." That's not quite what it measures.

## What TDP actually is

<Callout>
TDP (Thermal Design Power) is a guideline figure — originally intended to tell cooling solution manufacturers how much heat a cooler needs to be able to dissipate for the chip to run at its base clock reliably. It was never designed to be a precise real-world power consumption measurement.
</Callout>

## Why this distinction matters

- **Actual power draw varies by workload** — a CPU under light use draws far less than its TDP figure; under sustained heavy load with boost clocks active, it can draw meaningfully more than the stated TDP, depending on the specific chip and motherboard power limit settings.
- **TDP methodology differs between manufacturers and even between generations from the same manufacturer** — a stated TDP number isn't always calculated the same way across different chips, making direct cross-comparison less reliable than it appears.
- **Boost behavior specifically complicates this** — modern CPUs are designed to exceed their base clock (and often their TDP-implied power draw) for limited periods when thermal and power headroom allow, which is normal, intended behavior, not a specification violation.

## A concrete illustration

A CPU with a 65W TDP might draw close to that at its base clock under sustained load, but briefly draw significantly more during a boost period on a capable motherboard with generous power limits — this isn't the chip malfunctioning, it's the intended boost behavior working as designed, within safe thermal limits monitored by the CPU itself.

## What to actually check instead of relying on TDP alone

| If you want to know... | Check this instead of TDP alone |
|---|---|
| Real sustained power draw | Independent reviews measuring actual wattage under load |
| Whether your cooler is adequate | Reviews of that specific cooler with that specific (or similar) CPU |
| Expected temperatures | Thermal benchmark reviews, not the TDP spec sheet number |

## The practical takeaway

Treat TDP as a rough, historically useful reference for cooling requirements, not a precise power consumption spec you can use to directly compare two different chips' real-world efficiency. For that comparison, independent power-draw benchmarks are far more reliable than the spec sheet number alone.
