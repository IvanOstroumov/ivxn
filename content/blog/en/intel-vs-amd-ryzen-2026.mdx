The Intel vs AMD debate resets every generation, and last year's conclusion doesn't reliably carry over. Here's how to actually think about the decision instead of which brand to default to.

## The question that actually matters: what will you use it for?

<Callout>
"Which CPU is better" is the wrong question. "Which CPU is better for compiling large projects / gaming at 1440p / running a home server" gives you an actual answer — CPUs trade off differently across those workloads.
</Callout>

## For gaming

Games are sensitive to cache size and single-core performance more than raw core count. AMD's X3D line (extra 3D V-Cache stacked on the chip) tends to help disproportionately in cache-sensitive titles — simulation-heavy and open-world games especially. Intel's chips often clock higher, which helps in games that lean more on raw single-thread speed than cache. In practice, the difference at the top of each generation's lineup is usually a handful of percentage points either way — not the decisive gap the marketing implies.

## For development and compiling

Compiling large codebases, running multiple Docker containers, or running several local services at once benefits from **core count** more than from peak single-core clock speed — these are parallelizable workloads. This is generally where higher-core-count Ryzen chips do well for the price, though the exact numbers shift every generation, so check current benchmarks for your specific toolchain rather than assuming a fixed rule.

## For a home server / NAS-adjacent box

Power efficiency at idle and under light load matters more here than peak performance, since these machines often run 24/7. This varies chip to chip and generation to generation on both sides — again, check the specific SKU's idle power draw rather than assuming by brand.

## Comparison table (general tendencies, verify for your exact SKU)

| | Intel | AMD Ryzen |
|---|---|---|
| Gaming (cache-sensitive titles) | Competitive | Often strong (X3D chips) |
| Gaming (clock-sensitive titles) | Often strong (higher clocks) | Competitive |
| Compiling / multi-core workloads | Competitive | Often strong at a given price |
| Platform longevity (same motherboard across generations) | Historically shorter cycles | Historically longer cycles (AM4/AM5) |
| Integrated graphics (no discrete GPU) | Often stronger stock iGPU options | Improving, chip-dependent |

## Platform longevity is a real, underrated factor

AMD's AM4 socket lasted an unusually long time across several CPU generations, letting people upgrade the CPU without replacing the motherboard. AM5's longevity is still playing out. Intel has historically changed sockets more frequently. If you plan to upgrade the CPU alone in 2-3 years without a new motherboard, check the current state of socket longevity on both sides before buying — this changes faster than raw performance numbers do.

## The honest answer

Neither brand is categorically better in 2026 — the right choice depends on your actual workload and current-generation benchmarks, which shift every product cycle. If you're deciding for a specific use case (a build server, a gaming rig, a compact home server), that's a much easier question to answer concretely than "Intel or AMD" in the abstract.
